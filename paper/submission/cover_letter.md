Dear Editors,

We submit "Can open prescribing data detect effects of medicines on tuberculosis? An ecological
study of primary care and hospital prescribing and TB notifications in England, 2011–2024" for
consideration as a Research article.

Corticosteroids, biologics and other immunosuppressants increase an individual's risk of
tuberculosis. England publishes prescribing for every general practice, medicines issued by every
NHS trust, and TB notifications by local authority, so it is tempting to link them and look for
population-level signals. We tested whether that can work, and found that it cannot.

Three features of the paper may interest your readers.

1. **A negative result with a quantified explanation.** No medicine was associated with subsequent
   notifications after correction for multiple testing. We show why: the minimum detectable effect
   for oral glucocorticoids was 6.3% per 10% increase in prescribing, against 0.34% expected from
   published relative risks and 0.04–0.09% implied by national records of drug-associated TB. A
   permutation simulation on the real notification counts detected the published glucocorticoid
   effect no more often than no effect at all.

2. **Controls that test the linkage rather than assume it.** A disease-specific positive control
   (hospital treatment for active TB) tracked notifications but was recovered with an elasticity of
   only 0.36–0.40. A negative-control exposure showed inverse associations of the same size as
   candidate drugs, and prescribing in the following year predicted fewer notifications for several
   drug groups. These diagnostics are what distinguish residual confounding from drug effects, and
   they are rarely reported in ecological pharmaco-epidemiology.

3. **Full openness.** Every input is public. All analysis code, processed datasets, result files and
   figures are at https://github.com/drcjar/tb-prescribing-england, and the manuscript's tables are
   generated directly from the result files.

The work is reported following RECORD. It has not been published elsewhere and is not under
consideration by another journal. All data are aggregate and anonymous, so ethical approval was not
required. We have no competing interests and received no specific funding. Patients and the public
were not involved, as the study used published aggregate statistics.

We suggest the paper is handled under epidemiology or respiratory medicine, and we are happy to
propose reviewers with expertise in TB surveillance, pharmacoepidemiology and ecological study
methods.
